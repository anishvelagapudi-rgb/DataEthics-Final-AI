Research Brief

Can You Trust What You See Online?

Baylee Varnell • Gina Byon • Anish Velagapudi • Kenan Snider

Ms. Cantwell — Data Ethics In The Age Of AI — May 12, 2026

Statement of the Problem

Every day, millions of people scroll through news articles, videos, types of graphs, and social media posts that subtly distort information. Modern-day misinformation is no longer simply about completely false statements. Often, it appears through misleading statistics, manipulated graphing scales, missing context, emotionally charged headlines, or data presented in a way that changes how people perceive the truth. Because these tactics are difficult to recognize for the average person, many people consume this misinformation without ever noticing.

According to a 2024 Gallup poll, only 32% of Americans say they have a great deal or fair amount of trust in mass media. This growing distrust shows that people recognize the problems in the information they consume, but often cannot explain exactly why something feels misleading. Current fact-checking systems mainly focus on proving whether information is true or false — but they do not always explain how that information has been manipulated to influence people's opinions.

Our proposed invention is an AI-powered application that scans articles, videos, charts, and social media content in real time. The app identifies misleading framing, distorted statistics, manipulated graphs, and missing context that may otherwise fall through the cracks. It then explains the issues found to users in a clear and digestible way. Instead of only labeling information as false, the app teaches users how manipulation works and why content may be deceptive. By increasing media literacy and helping users think critically about digital information, TruthLens aims to rebuild trust and transparency in online communication.

Supporting Research

Finding 1

Nearly Half of All Adults Encounter Misinformation Every Single Day

The first finding examines the percentage of adults who believe they see misinformation on a daily basis. This is essential for understanding the scale of the problem: if people cannot reliably determine whether the information they consume is accurate, they may reject correct information and continue to make poorly-informed decisions.

According to Statista, 46% of adults report that they encounter misinformation on a daily basis. This widespread uncertainty demonstrates a clear need for tools that help the majority of the population evaluate what they read. TruthLens would directly address this by providing accurate contextual information to users and flagging whether content consumed through various media is accurately represented.

Finding 2

Misinformation Threatens Elections, Public Health, and Democratic Integrity

The second finding focuses on the real-world effects of widespread misinformation. Understanding the negative consequences is essential: without demonstrable harm, a tool like TruthLens would have limited justification. The most significant field in which misinformation causes documented damage is political environments. According to Deakin University's library guide on misinformation: "Misinformation can distort elections, manipulate public opinion, and threaten the integrity of democratic processes." This highlights serious, systemic concerns caused by the unchecked spread of false or misleading information.

Beyond politics, the consequences of misinformation during emergencies are severe. A global pandemic provides a clear example: misinformation about safety measures, treatment options, and transmission routes can cost lives and waste critical resources. TruthLens's real-time scanning capability is particularly valuable in these high-stakes, fast-moving information environments where deliberate misinformation spreads fastest and where the cost of believing it is highest.

Brief Description of the Invention

TruthLens is an AI tool designed to detect misinformation across various media types. It is capable of analyzing images, text, and audio for a complete range of content. By educating users on what is false and providing the accurate context around the media they are consuming, TruthLens aims to greatly reduce the amount of accepted misinformation in public discourse. Over time, as users internalize the patterns TruthLens highlights, this would result in a broader decrease in the attempted spread of misinformation — because it would become less effective.

TruthLens will also explain the causes of misleading information to increase individuals' media literacy and help create a more critically-informed society. The business model is flexible: it could be offered as a free, ad-supported product or a subscription-based service. With conservative numbers — just 1% of the 331 million US internet users as active users — the ad-supported model generates approximately $3 million annually. A premium tier at $5 per month could push annual revenue toward $13–14 million, given that subscription users generate 4.6× the revenue of ad-only users. The primary goal, however, is not profit but access: ensuring that everyone has this tool available to them.

Policy Implications

This research highlights the growing need for digital transparency and media literacy in modern society. Policymakers could use findings from tools like TruthLens to encourage clearer standards for online advertising and data visualization. Schools and public institutions could use the technology to teach critical thinking and responsible media consumption. However, significant regulatory challenges exist: AI systems must avoid political bias and remain fully transparent about how content is analyzed. Governments and technology companies would need to establish guidelines ensuring these systems remain fair, accurate, and — critically — are not used to censor opinions. With the right oversight, tools like TruthLens could help build a more informed public and reduce the spread of manipulative digital content.
